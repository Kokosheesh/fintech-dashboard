The qualities of a great founder

Kohinoor Mukherjee · 15 November 2026

Most lists of founder qualities read like a horoscope: passionate, resilient, visionary. Everyone recognises themselves, so the list teaches nothing. A better test is to ask what a great founder does differently on an ordinary Tuesday, when cash is tight and no option is clean.

They decide with incomplete information. A founder rarely gets the data they want before a call has to be made. Great ones set a deadline for the decision, name what would change their mind, and move. Waiting for certainty is itself a decision, usually the most expensive one.

They know their runway in months, not rupees. Cash in the bank means little on its own. Great founders think in burn and runway, and they know which hire, launch or discount moves that number. They can tell you, without opening a spreadsheet, how many months they have left and what happens if the next round slips by a quarter.

They hire for the next stage, not the last one. The people who got you to your first ten customers are not always the people who get you to your first hundred. Great founders make that call early and kindly, before the company pays for avoiding it.

They separate a bad decision from a bad outcome. A sound bet can lose; a reckless one can win. Great founders review decisions on what they knew at the time, so they learn the right lesson instead of the lucky one.

They protect focus. Every partnership, feature request and side market looks like growth. Great founders say no to most of them, because start-ups die from too many good ideas more often than from too few.

They tell the board the truth early. Bad news shared in month three is a problem to solve together. Shared in month nine, it is a crisis and a question of trust.

None of these are traits you are born with. They are habits, and habits come from repetition. That is why Founder’s Seat exists: thirty-six months of decisions where every call compounds, so you can practise judgement before it costs a real company.

How to be a great single-employee founder with AI agents

Kohinoor Mukherjee · 15 November 2026

A company of one used to mean a freelancer. With AI agents, one person can now run research, support, content and code that once needed a small team. The constraint is no longer hands; it is judgement. Doing it well takes a different set of habits.

Be the manager, not the operator. Treat each agent like a new hire: a clear brief, the context it needs, examples of good work and a definition of done. Vague instructions produce confident, polished and wrong output.

Keep the decisions human. Let agents draft, summarise, test and compare. Keep pricing, hiring, money and anything a customer will read under your own sign-off. An agent can widen your options; it should not choose among them for you.

Build checks, not blind trust. Agents fail quietly. Add a review step for anything that leaves the building, track errors in one place, and turn every repeated correction into a better instruction.

Write things down. Your processes are now prompts. A short playbook for each recurring task keeps agents consistent and lets you switch tools without starting over. Keep a decision log too: what you chose, why, and what the agents told you.

Spend the saved time on customers. The hours agents give back only matter if you put them into the work only you can do: talking to users, making the hard calls and saying no to distractions.

That last skill, deciding under pressure when no tool can decide for you, is exactly what Founder’s Seat trains.
